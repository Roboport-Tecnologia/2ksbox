Games of the era were drawn for a CRT monitor. Their pixels were meant
to blur into each other a little, with scanlines and a glow. On a flat
panel, stretched to fit, they look blocky and soft at the same time.

2ksbox takes the machine's own picture, at its own resolution, and runs
it through a CRT shader before it reaches your screen. The shaders come
from [libretro's collection](https://github.com/libretro/slang-shaders),
the same ones [RetroArch](https://www.retroarch.com) uses.

## Shader profiles

A *shader profile* is a shader preset plus your own adjustments to it,
under a name. The download on first start adds three:

- **CRT Aperture**, a sharp aperture-grille look. It's the default.
- **CRT Royale**, a detailed CRT with a phosphor mask and glow. It needs
  a strong graphics card and looks better with a custom configuration.
- **Apple II**, for something different.

Open **Shader Profiles** to see them. The switch in the **Default**
column marks the profile that every machine set to "(default)" uses.
**No default** clears it, and those machines then show the picture with
no shader.

## Make your own

<Steps>

1. In **Shader Profiles**, click **New**.
2. Type a **Name** and pick a **Preset (.slangp)**. The collection has
   hundreds, in folders by kind. The `crt` folder is the place to start.
3. Pick a **Preview image**: a screenshot of a game you play. The
   preview shows it through the shader as you change things. The
   **Scale** slider shows it at the size you'll play at.
4. Adjust the preset's parameters until it looks right.
5. Click **Save**.

</Steps>

A good preview image is one the player saved with Ctrl+Alt+S (⌥⌘S on
macOS). It is the machine's own picture before the shader.

## Pick a profile for a machine

In the machine's settings, on the **Display** page, set **Shader
profile**. "(default)" follows whatever profile you marked as the
default, so changing the default changes every machine set to it.

A Windows 11 machine starts with no shader, because a modern desktop is
meant for a flat panel.

## How the player scales the picture

The player keeps the machine's picture in proportion and adds black
bars rather than stretching it. 320×200 games get the 4:3 shape they had
on a monitor.

The *View* menu's **Scale** chooses the size:

- **Largest That Fits** fills the window as far as whole steps allow.
- **1x** to **4x** show each of the machine's pixels as exactly that
  many of yours. Retina screens have more steps. A size that doesn't fit
  the window is cropped.
- **Fit Window to Picture** sizes the window to the picture.

Whole steps matter for a CRT shader: its scanlines land on the same rows
of your screen every time, so the picture doesn't shimmer.

<Aside type="tip">
**Full Screen** (Ctrl+Alt+Shift+F, or the green button on macOS) with
**Largest That Fits** is the closest to sitting in front of the old
monitor.
</Aside>
